Chapter I · The IslandWarhammer Armies: Albion

What the Old Ones Left

The Old Ones raised their pylons across the whole of Albion, a single island-wide work meant to hold Chaos off the land. Then they left. What they built did not stop.

The Ancients stayed on as custodians: long-lived, humanoid, charged with the keeping of the system. They are not the Old Ones and should not be mistaken for them.

The Fimir came after, and humans after that — the latest arrivals on an island already old and already running.

Argant, and What Came After

Argant was the founding king, and a conquering one. He drove the Fimir toward the salt coasts. Everything after that is a tangle of record and embellishment, and the two should be kept apart where they can be.

A machine remembered as a faith

The pylons still work. Nobody fully understands them. The knowledge that keeps them running has come down as druidic craft — ritual, arcana, inherited routine — which is to say degraded engineering preserved as tradition, and none the less effective for it.

The High Church reads Argant and the distant past as saintly and divine; the older folk faith reads them otherwise; the pylons go on working regardless of either.

Not the Sleeping King Albion's returning-king story belongs to someone else. Argant and the Sleeping King are not the same man and must not be run together.

The IslandThe pylons

The Pylons

Half page — awaiting art Standing stones on a wet hillside, runic wiring still legible half-h-pylon-site.webp

The greatest of the works still show their runic wiring, cut deep enough to read after everything else has gone. The smaller and older sites do not. They have been swallowed: into barrows, into witch huts, into caves and sheep folds, into standing stones, hill forts and ruined masonry nobody has questioned in generations.

A farmer may hold a pylon in his back field and call it a wall.

The IslandWhat the pylons attract

What Gathers at the Stones

Wolves den around old pylon sites. This is not an omen and nobody treats it as one; it is simply where the wolves are, reliably enough that a hunter can plan around it.

Where the work fails

A minor pylon, or one that has drifted out of true, can open. What comes through is Beastmen. A misaligned stone in an empty field is worth more attention than a grand one still doing its job.

Who is responsible

The Ancients hold the duty for the system. Most human readings of the stones — pious, folk, or otherwise — have the origin wrong, which does not stop the maintenance from being done correctly.

The IslandThe Ancients

The Ancients

They are the old custodians: humanoid, long-lived, left behind to keep the work running. They are not the Old Ones, and they are not all benevolent, and they are not all intact. Some are asleep. Some are eccentric. Some have been damaged by the sheer length of their own lives.

The Trickster

One of them is associated with impossible knots, with road junctions, and with gifts — and, in time, with roundabouts. He is erratic and he is not safe to plan around.

The IslandSarfareth

Sarfareth, Who Speaks of Snakes

Sarfareth is an Ancient, a Rememberer, and the Snakespeaker: one figure under three names, and the chief antagonist of the old system. He is bound up with Argant, and the binding runs both ways.

The argument underneath

The long conflict on Albion is not really about territory. It is about whether the machinery should be kept running as it is, or understood, or taken hold of. The Ancients have one answer. The living kings keep arriving at others.

One figure Sarfareth, the Snakespeaker and He Who Speaks of Snakes are the same person. Confirmed by the creator, 19 October 2026.

The IslandChurch and custom

Church, Folk and Fold

Albion keeps a High Church and an older folk faith at the same time, in the same villages, often in the same people. The saintly Argant and the Argant of the hearth stories are both attended to, and nobody finds the arrangement strange.

Reformed stone churches sometimes seat their councils at round tables. Whose idea that was first is not agreed, and the argument is itself a tradition now.

The shield turned over A commander's Argant round shield, laid face-down, is a map table. The marks on the back of an old shield are often more interesting than the paint on the front.

The IslandThe texture of the place

An Island of Routine

Albion is ancient, wet and barrow-strewn, and its people are not living in a legend. They are living in institutions, politics, superstitions and inherited routine, all of which matter at least as much as the battles.

Traditions here are load-bearing. A custom is often the last working part of something older, kept going by people who would be insulted to hear it called maintenance.

Bronze, not plate

The material culture is Bronze Age and Britonnic: barrows, hoards, round shields, worked metal. No high-medieval armour anywhere on the island.

Regions The Grey Coast, Forest and Fenn, and broad areas of Red and Blue influence. These are spheres, not borders.

Chapter IIThe four hosts

The Four Hosts

The hosts are overlapping cultural, political and magical factions. They are not four nations with four borders, and a man may stand inside more than one of them depending on who is asking and what day it is.

The Four HostsThe Blue Host

Alfran Longbritches

Longshanks to some, and Shortsword when somebody wants to needle him. Ambitious, modernising, and openly interested in ideas from abroad — which on this island is itself a position. Tartan trousers, high boots, a moustache, and no interest at all in looking antiquarian.

He rides Shaggy, a long creature out of a loch, with a throne saddle built onto its back.

The host

Painted warriors and javelin troops, war dogs, the Waiting Guard, javelin ram-riders, and the giant Tam Loggins as an ally.

The court

His younger brother Elfred Sidesaddle keeps a Court of Antlers and rides a deer sideways. The Beast guards it: an imposing figure out of an Araby prince's court, with a stolen purple gemstone set in his belly button, who follows court politics closely and will put himself between Elfred and trouble without being asked.

Roundabouts Alfran favours standardisation and accountability, roundabouts included, and would like the old sources of power rationalised.

The Four HostsThe Red Host

Maddoc the Flame

She leads the Red Host.

The host she leads

Red fights as spectacle. Fire breathers and ember druids go ahead of the line; painted slingers and fire maidens work the flanks; boar chariots come through where the ground allows. Behind them the Shrikers — women with long polearms, who sound like the end of something.

The host's centre is a bronze chariot drawn by she-lions.

The Bloodflame sorceress She rides a cart to war. Whether she is the Bloodmaiden or the Bloodflame, and whether the cart is drawn by ox or boar, is not settled.

This is heat and ritual and nerve, not a cult and not a darkness. Red is the loudest host on the field and means to be.

The Four HostsGreen

Robin Fair

Half page — awaiting art Woodsmen in paper fox masks, reed boats on a dyke half-h-fennfolk-reed-boats.webp

Outlaw and folk-king of Forest and Fenn, famously handsome and well aware of it. He dresses prehistoric rather than medieval, carries a nine-inch bronze blade that everyone has heard of, and will not get on a horse. His height is unexceptional, which his own people blame on the vegetarianism.

Big John goes with him: very tall, very gentle, and the reason most arguments end early.

The Four HostsForest and Fenn

Forest and Fenn

Green is Albion's default earthy world, and the host fights like the country it comes from: Dykejumpers over the channels, woodsmen in paper fox masks, short willow bows, reed boats where there is no path at all. Bog imps and will-o'-wisps come along whether invited or not.

Root and Knots

Green's casters go by that name.

The witches

Jenny Willows works from a boat with a lantern and a trail of smoky wisps, and has one arm long enough to row with. The Knot Wife carries a cane of impossible knots and keeps a frog.

The Four HostsThe Grey Host

The Grey Host

Grey has no leader. It is the older supernatural Albion — coast, barrows, weather, and the drowned, sleeping and undying — and it arrives rather than musters.

Old Shannok

A blind wizard with white eyes and long nails, carried on a moving throne of stones joined by vines. The stones hang in place. They do not rotate.

The Wild Hunt

It rides a boat-chariot behind an undying king. The retinue is his wife, a wizard, a crone and two demi-giant sons; the beasts are lion, stag, unicorn and boar.

With them come giants, coast hags, kelpies — seaweed horses fused with their riders — the drowned Waiting Guard, black hounds, and Ancients.

Not all dead, not all hostile Grey is old, not evil. Treating every figure in it as undead gets the host wrong.
Canon to confirm When Longshanks steals the Hunt's horn, and how.

Chapter III · The FimirBroods and brood mothers

The Fimir

The Fimir live in broods under brood mothers, and a brood mother grows more magical the older she gets. The magic does not arrive from outside. It grows out of keeping the small hearth — a lifetime of ordinary skill practised so long and so well that it stops being ordinary.

Who goes first

Age decides precedence. Who is older matters, and is known. Birthdays matter, and so does giving presents on the days that count. Rival broods feud, and only rarely take it to a queen.

The FimirSkallies and flint

Failed Broods

A brood that fails can produce Skallies: itinerant, ghoul-like and bleak, carrying flint knives and stones, and hungry. They eat eggs. They are not collectors of curiosities and they are not cooks, and writing them that way misses what they are.

Music will sometimes hold a band of Skallies together, at a level below thought. Beyond that they are desperate, and very hard to help.

Flint

Flint weaponry is the distinctive material culture, and it is a choice rather than a poverty.

The lidless eye A jewel-encrusted eye, kept wet with brackish water by an attending witch. Its origin is not settled.
In the field One-eyed fishlike warriors with musicians, standards and leaders, and the Picked-Over King carried by his retainers.

Chapter IV · Albion Day to DayAraby and ring-money

Araby, and the Count of Rings

Albion thinks of Araby as wealth made visible: courts, harems, pillows, tigers and lions, giant snails, jewellery, and objects that do things. The picture is part true and wholly envied. Alfran's foreign ideas come from that direction, and they are ideas, not curiosities.

1 ringOne gold ring, the base unit
1 amuletFive rings
1 crownThirteen amulets, or sixty-five rings

Under the spectacle this is a working monetary system with standard values, and it is accounted for as one.

Albion Day to DayCustom, sport and blackweed

Blackweed and the Saltwater Cup

Blackweed is brewed, chewed and smoked, and does the work of tea, tobacco and a mild drink all at once. Most households keep some. Most arguments are conducted over it.

The Saltwater Cup

Albion's rough running-and-handling game. Stubby captains a Saltman side: seventh son of a seventh son, father of six boys, and entirely content with the name.

Sport, minor titles, gestures, craft, coinage, presents and food are part of the island, not decoration on it.
