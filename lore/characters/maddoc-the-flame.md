# Maddoc the Flame

They call her the Flame, though the name belongs as much to the country she commands as to the woman herself. In the lands of the Red Host, fires are lit to mark births and victories, disputes and funerals; the smoke of a thousand hearths hangs over the valleys even in summer. Maddoc has learned to make a kingdom out of those scattered flames, and to turn its fierce, quarrelsome people in one direction—at least for as long as she chooses to lead them.

Maddoc is one of three sisters. Bredda, broad and formidable, commands the Bronze Guard, whose armour is made from overlapping hammered brasses bearing the everyday emblems of cattle, horses and suns. Gentle Helda has little taste for war and tends her three lionesses, binding herself to the favourite when their strength is needed. The sisters are unlike one another in almost every respect. Their differences have not made Maddoc's rule simple, but they have made her court impossible to mistake for anybody else's.

The Red Host still musters its Painted Warriors, much as the other peoples of Albion do. Yet under Maddoc's command there are fighting companies seldom encountered elsewhere: the shrieking spear-women with their vicious birds, the ragged Skulds with their burning oil, the fire-breathing Maidens and the dreadful Widows who push their husbands' funeral carts into battle. Great wicker figures are hauled forward by sweating crews and set ablaze when the time comes. Even the war machines seem more like dares than devices: a tree bent to fling burning peat, or barrels of pitch stacked high and released in one terrible rush. Not every invention works twice. That has seldom discouraged anyone.

At court, the sorceress Bloodflame serves as Maddoc's chief wizard, though the wizard has a cult and ambitions of her own. Maddoc is no devotee of that cult. Her authority is her own, maintained by alliances, spectacle, courage and the ordinary danger of disappointing her. Everyone at her court wants something; the queen is merely better placed than most to decide when to grant it.

Maddoc's own chariot is built for speed, hung with red cloth and fierce totems rather than cumbersome flags. Two trained male lions pull it—splendid, maned beasts under no enchantment. Beside her rides her personal Oiler, the war-painter and intimate attendant who bears a round bronze shield to turn aside blows and arrows, and a short sword for the moments when the fighting closes. The queen's unusual conventions of virginity are a matter for her household, not her enemies. On the battlefield, nobody mistakes her attendant's gentler duties for an absence of courage.

When the lions come on, there is no mistaking Maddoc either. She stands high with spear raised, hair loose in the wind, while shields splinter and the line ahead begins to give way. The Red Host follows with a roar, in equal parts loyalty, delight and alarm. For a few heartbeats it seems possible that the whole world might catch fire.
